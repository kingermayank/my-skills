# Brief, research, and strategy

## Reverse the brief before designing

Extract the client's stated needs, the audience experience they want to change, commercial goals, offer, current evidence, constraints, applications, and definition of success. Separate those from your interpretation and open questions. Resolve contradictory adjectives through observable behaviour: what would “confident but welcoming” mean on a sign, during service, or in an error message?

A reverse brief should say: the problem; who it affects; desired feeling and action; competitive context; proposed strategic questions; scope and deliverables; constraints; what needs confirming. Do not silently commit the client to budgets, schedules, audiences, or values. If the user only wants a reply or proposal outline, produce that instead of a full strategy.

## Investigate in three passes

1. **Discover:** start with known competitors, validate relevance, and add direct and adjacent alternatives. Around five is a useful initial sample, not an exhaustiveness claim.
2. **Compare:** inspect actual visual and verbal material side by side. Record offer, target audience, positioning, message themes, voice, visual codes, emotional promise, and customer experience. Keep source URLs and retrieval dates.
3. **Interpret:** identify repeated themes, category conventions, and unserved emotional or experiential needs. Mark interpretations as hypotheses; absence from a small sample is not proof that nobody owns a territory.

Combine visual observation with sourced text. An AI summary of a website does not establish what the brand looks like. Inspect screenshots or examples before making design claims. Report sampling and access limitations.

## Gather audience language before synthesising it

Use available customer feedback, interviews, surveys, observed behaviour, reviews, and relevant discussions. For a new brand, competitor audiences can supply research leads; state the substitution. Preserve the link between a finding and its source, date, and context. Quote only the minimum needed, within source-use limits.

Separate raw observations from interpretation. Cluster repeated needs, frustrations, motivations, rituals, cultural signals, and desired emotional outcomes. Include counterexamples. Demographics alone rarely resolve creative decisions. A useful synthesis is:

| Evidence | Pattern or tension | Audience need | Creative implication | Confidence / next validation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Do not infer prevalence from a few vivid complaints. A synthetic persona, imagined quote, or designer intuition is a hypothesis rather than observed customer evidence. When browsing is unavailable, use supplied material and label research gaps.

## Position the brand

Offer distinct strategic possibilities before choosing language. For each, identify the audience, emotional need, promise, difference, supporting proof, and likely creative implications. A useful working sentence connects: **brand/category + audience + meaningful benefit + distinctive approach**. It is an internal decision tool; it need not read like a slogan.

Stress-test: is the promise believable? Is it specific enough to exclude an alternative? Does it answer audience evidence? Can the business deliver it? Would another competitor say the same thing? Does it suggest coherent behaviour and execution?

For example, promising fast, considerate service while adopting a deliberately abrasive personality creates a real tension. Decide where wit ends and exclusion begins; do not rationalise mistreating the audience as brand differentiation.

## Turn positioning into usable pillars

Develop roughly 3–5 emotional, behavioural, or attitudinal principles. For each, write its meaning, evidence or rationale, a visual implication, a verbal implication, a behavioural implication, and a boundary. Avoid a list of interchangeable values such as innovation, quality, and authenticity without a distinctive interpretation.

Test the pillar against at least one concrete choice. If it cannot help decide between two options, make it more useful. Record an approved core strategy separately from unselected possibilities.

## Present a strategic argument

Connect problem and context → audience evidence → competitive patterns → opportunity → positioning → pillars → voice/naming → proposed next steps. Distinguish observed findings from the recommendation. Include only research that changes the decision; a long research dump makes the argument harder to assess.

When visual identity is still undecided, a restrained presentation can help people judge the strategy without confusing a speculative deck aesthetic with a proposed brand direction. Use deliberate hierarchy and clear comparisons without prematurely locking the identity.
